## §1. THRESHOLD REQUIREMENTS

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- Is there a **writing**?
- Is testator **18+ and sound mind**?
- Is the document **revocable** during lifetime?
</aside>

### 1.1 Who May Make a Will

Under **PA 2501**, any person who is 18 years of age or older and of sound mind may make a will. The UPC follows a similar baseline requirement, establishing 18 as the minimum age for testamentary capacity across most jurisdictions.

### 1.2 What Is a Will?

**UPC 1-201(56)** provides a comprehensive definition: "Will" includes codicil and any testamentary instrument that merely appoints an executor, revokes or revises another will, nominates a guardian, or expressly excludes or limits the right of an individual or class to succeed to property of the decedent passing by intestate succession.

A **codicil** is an amendment to a will that supplements or revises the original document without replacing it entirely. Critically, a codicil must satisfy the same formalities required for will execution. A codicil never replaces the entirety of another will; it either adds to it, supplementing the will, or substituting a small portion of the will.

**UPC 2-602** establishes the scope of a will's reach: "A will may provide for the passage of all property the testator owns at death and all property acquired by the decedent's estate after the testator's death." This means a will can effectively dispose of after-acquired property, even property that comes into the estate after death.

The **key distinguishing feature** of a will is that it creates no present rights in any beneficiary. All rights are contingent upon the testator's death. Therefore, the testator must retain the right to revoke the will or change it via codicil during their lifetime. If a document creates present, irrevocable rights, it is not a will but rather some other form of transfer.

## §2. TESTAMENTARY INTENT

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- Does document show intent to **control dispositions at death**?
- Does it use **dispositive language** ("give," "devise," "bequeath," "inherit")?
- Is it labeled as a will/codicil?
- Can **extrinsic evidence** establish intent?
</aside>

### 2.1 Core Test

Testamentary intent requires that the testator intended the document to control dispositions at death and create no rights until death occurs. The intent must be that the document have testamentary effect—that is, the document should operate as the testator's will upon their death.

Extrinsic evidence is admissible to establish testamentary intent, particularly in the case of holographic wills. Under **UPC 2-502(c)**, intent that a document constitute the testator's will can be established by extrinsic evidence, including, for holographic wills, portions of the document that are not in the testator's handwriting.

### 2.2 Key Cases

**Estate of Kuralt**: Charles Kuralt sent a letter to his companion Shannon stating that he would "have the lawyer visit the hospital to be sure you **inherit** the rest of the place in MIT, if it comes to that." He underlined the word "inherit." The court found this letter demonstrated testamentary intent. Although Kuralt intended to formalize the transfer through a lawyer, the use of the term "inherit"—underlined by Kuralt—reflected his intention to make a posthumous disposition of the property. No testator would use the word "inherit" as a mere statement of future intent; it is inherently testamentary language. The context (sent from a hospital, knowing death was possible) combined with the dispositive language established that this was a codicil to his existing will.

**Edmundson v. Fountain**: Oral Fountain left a handwritten document titled "last will" that listed her children's names and certain property under each name. The document was signed and witnessed, but the court held it failed as a will because it lacked any dispositive language. Simply listing property next to names, even in a document labeled "last will," is insufficient. The lesson is clear: you need both an appropriate label AND dispositive content. Merely organizing information about who you'd like to benefit is not the same as expressing testamentary intent to transfer property at death.

## §3. TESTAMENTARY CAPACITY (THE CUNNINGHAM TEST)

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- Does testator know **nature and extent of property**?
- Does testator know **natural objects of bounty**?
- Does testator understand **disposition being made**?
- Does testator understand **relationship among elements**?
- Any **mental illness**? (Illness alone ≠ incapacity)
- Any **insane delusions** affecting disposition?
- Was testator in a **lucid interval** when executing?
</aside>

### 3.1 Four-Part Test (The Cunningham Test)

To possess testamentary capacity, a testator must know and understand:

1. **The nature and extent of their property**: The testator must have a general understanding of what they own. This does not require knowledge of exact dollar values or precise inventories. Rather, knowledge of the categories of assets that compose the estate is sufficient. For example, knowing "I own a house, some bank accounts, and stocks" satisfies this element, even if the testator cannot recite account balances or stock valuations.
2. **The natural objects of their bounty**: The testator must know who the people are in their life who would ordinarily be expected to benefit from their estate—typically family members and loved ones in their orbit. This requires awareness of close relationships and an understanding of who depends on them or has a natural claim to their affection and support.
3. **The disposition being made**: The testator must have a general understanding of their estate plan—what they are giving, to whom, and in what proportions. Perfect recall of every detail is not required, but the testator must understand the basic structure and substance of their testamentary plan.
4. **The relationship among the first three elements**: This is the synthesizing requirement. The testator must understand how their property, their loved ones, and their planned disposition all fit together. In other words, they must be able to connect the dots and comprehend how their wishes regarding property distribution relate to the people they know and the assets they own.

### 3.2 Key Principles

**Mental illness does not equal incapacity**. A person may suffer from diagnosed psychiatric conditions, yet still possess sufficient testamentary capacity. The question is not whether the testator has a mental illness, but whether that illness interferes with their ability to understand the elements of the Cunningham test at the time of will execution.

**Lucid intervals validate wills**. Even if a testator's capacity fluctuates—fading in and out of competence—a will executed during a lucid interval is valid. The law does not require continuous, unwavering capacity; it requires capacity at the moment of execution.

**Insane delusion** is a specific false belief held without any rational basis in fact, maintained even in the face of all evidence to the contrary. If an insane delusion affects a specific provision in the will, that provision is void. If the delusion affects the entire testamentary plan, the entire will fails. The key inquiry is whether the false belief materially influenced the testamentary disposition.

**Burden of proof**: The party contesting the will on grounds of lack of capacity bears the burden of proving incapacity. The law presumes capacity, and the contestant must overcome that presumption.

### 3.3 Critical Cases

**In re Estate of Romero**: Robert Romero suffered from schizophrenia and was under a Veterans Administration guardianship, yet the court upheld his will. The court emphasized several key points:

First, evidence of mental illness alone is not sufficient to prove lack of testamentary capacity. While Romero had been treated for schizophrenia, this diagnosis did not automatically disqualify him from making a valid will.

Second, the testator need not know the exact value of their estate. Knowledge of the categories of assets is sufficient. When Romero executed his will, his estate was valued at approximately $90,000. At his death, it had grown to approximately $450,000. This increase in value did not matter because Romero understood the general nature and extent of his assets at the time of execution—he knew he owned property, had savings, and possessed other assets.

Third, Romero's attorney provided powerful testimony about Romero's rationality during their multiple meetings about the will. The attorney testified that Romero was of sound mind and understood the consequences of his actions when he signed the will.

Fourth, the court recognized that disinheriting children can be entirely rational when based on the care received versus neglect experienced. Romero chose to leave only $500 to each of his children and the remainder of his estate to his mother and sister because of his mother and sister's love and care and the lack thereof from his children. This was a reasoned decision, not evidence of incapacity.

**Key holding**: **Capacity is distinct from volition**. These are separate inquiries for capacity versus undue influence. Understanding the terms of your will does not guarantee that you exercised free will in creating those terms. A testator might have perfect capacity to understand what they're doing while simultaneously being unduly influenced by another person.

**In re Estate of Strittmater**: Miss Strittmater never married and lived with her parents. After their deaths, she wrote vicious letters calling her father "a corrupt, vicious, unintelligent savage" and her mother "that moronic she devil." She engaged in bizarre behavior like smashing a family clock and killing the family cat. Yet her attorney testified that over years of working with her, she was entirely reasonable and normal in their interactions.

The court held that eccentricity does not equal insanity. Freedom of testation is very broad, allowing testators to make dispositions that others might view as unjust or capricious. If an individual possesses testamentary capacity, they may make their will as idiosyncratic as their desires dictate, within very broad limits. The key question is whether the disposition was caused by an insane delusion. Here, Strittmater's unusual personality and strange behaviors did not rise to the level of insane delusion affecting her testamentary capacity. Her will was upheld.

## §4. UNDUE INFLUENCE (UI) — CRITICAL EXAM TOPIC

<aside>
✅

- If confidential relationship + suspicious circumstances → **Presumption of UI**
- Burden **shifts** to proponent (beneficiary)
- Proponent must prove by **clear and convincing evidence** will reflects free wishes

**Step 5: Can proponent rebut?** (Defenses)

- Capacity witnesses present?
- Prior relationship justifies disposition?
- **Public execution** (bank, neutral location)?
- **Independent legal advice**?
- Testator signed outside beneficiary's presence?
</aside>

### 4.1 Definition

Undue influence occurs when a wrongdoer substitutes their will for the testator's will through coercion or domination that overcomes the testator's volition. This is fundamentally different from capacity—a testator may have perfect understanding of what they are doing (capacity) while being coerced into doing it (undue influence). The essence of undue influence is the destruction of free agency.

### 4.2 Proving UI: Two Methods

### Method A: Direct Evidence (Rare)

In rare cases, a contestant can present direct evidence of actual coercion, threats, or manipulation. This might include testimony from witnesses who observed the wrongdoer pressuring the testator, recorded conversations, or other direct proof of improper influence.

### Method B: Circumstantial Evidence (Common) — Restatement §8.3

The far more common approach is to prove undue influence through circumstantial evidence. Under this method, the contestant **MUST PROVE** two elements:

1. **Confidential relationship** PLUS
2. **Suspicious circumstances**

If both elements are proven, a **presumption of undue influence arises** and the burden shifts to the proponent of the will (typically the beneficiary accused of exercising undue influence) to rebut the presumption.

### 4.3 CONFIDENTIAL RELATIONSHIP (3 Types)

The Restatement Third of Property §8.3 recognizes that the single term "confidential relationship" embraces three sometimes distinct relationships:

**1. Fiduciary Relationship**: This arises from a settled category of fiduciary obligation. Some fiduciary relationships involve hired professionals: an attorney is in a fiduciary relationship with their client; an agent under a power of attorney is in a fiduciary relationship with the principal; a trustee is in a fiduciary relationship with the trust beneficiary. Other fiduciary relationships may exist between family members, such as when an adult child holds power of attorney for an aging parent, even though the agent receives no fee for acting in that capacity.

**2. Reliant Relationship**: This is a question of fact determined by the circumstances. The contestant must establish that a relationship existed based on special trust and confidence—for example, that the testator was accustomed to be guided by the judgment or advice of the alleged wrongdoer, or was justified in placing confidence in the belief that the alleged wrongdoer would act in the interest of the testator. This often exists between a testator and a trusted advisor, close friend, or family member who the testator relies upon for guidance in important decisions.

**3. Dominant-Subservient Relationship**: This is also a question of fact. The contestant must establish that the testator was subservient to the alleged wrongdoer's dominant influence or control. Such a relationship might exist between a hired caregiver and an ill or feeble donor, or between an adult child and an ill or feeble parent. The key is the testator's dependence on the alleged wrongdoer, often for physical care, emotional support, or basic daily needs.

**Note**: In any particular case, these three relationships might overlap. For example, an adult child with power of attorney (fiduciary) who also serves as primary caregiver for a disabled parent (dominant-subservient) while the parent relies on the child's judgment for all major decisions (reliant) would satisfy all three categories simultaneously.

### 4.4 SUSPICIOUS CIRCUMSTANCES (6 Key Factors)

<aside>
🚩

**Count these factors explicitly in your exam answer**

</aside>

The existence of a confidential relationship alone is not sufficient to raise a presumption of undue influence. There must also be suspicious circumstances surrounding the preparation, execution, or formulation of the will. Courts examine six key factors:

**1. Beneficiary's participation in procurement**: This is one of the most damning factors. Did the beneficiary contact the lawyer on behalf of the testator? Did the beneficiary attend drafting meetings? Did the beneficiary participate in discussions about the terms of the will? The factor becomes **most suspicious when the beneficiary's own lawyer** drafted the will or when the beneficiary actively participated in shaping its terms. This suggests the will may reflect the beneficiary's wishes rather than the testator's.

**2. Secrecy and haste**: Was the will hidden from other family members? Was execution rushed? Was there concealment of the testator's location during execution? These circumstances suggest the beneficiary sought to prevent others from learning about or potentially interfering with the testamentary plan, which implies consciousness of wrongdoing.

**3. Deviation from prior plan**: Did the new will dramatically change the distribution scheme from prior wills? For example, if a prior will left shares of 40/30/30 to three children, but a new will leaves 80/10/10, this radical shift raises questions. Courts compare provisions in successive wills to determine whether the changes are so dramatic as to warrant suspicion.

**4. Unwarranted or unfair benefit**: Does the beneficiary receive a benefit that is disproportionate to their relationship with the testator? This is especially suspicious if the beneficiary's gain comes at the expense of natural objects of the testator's bounty who previously had good relationships with the testator. If a caregiver hired six months before death receives the entire estate while children who cared for the testator for decades receive nothing, this disproportion raises a red flag.

**5. Testator susceptibility**: Was the testator suffering from depression, grief, or illness at the time of execution? Was the testator in declining health with constant care needs? Was the testator dependent on the beneficiary for daily functions? Look for evidence suggesting the testator "could not do anything without" the alleged wrongdoer. Physical or mental weakness makes a testator more vulnerable to influence and manipulation.

**6. Lack of independent advice**: Did the testator have no attorney, or did they use the beneficiary's lawyer? Was the beneficiary present during attorney meetings, preventing the testator from speaking freely? Independent legal advice is one of the strongest protections against undue influence. Its absence is correspondingly suspicious. If the testator met privately with their own attorney who had no connection to the beneficiary, this factor weighs against a finding of undue influence. But if the beneficiary selected the attorney, paid the attorney, or sat in on all meetings, the testator did not receive truly independent advice.

### 4.5 Burden-Shifting Framework

Understanding the burden-shifting framework is critical for analyzing undue influence claims:

**Step 1**: The contestant (person challenging the will) bears the initial burden of proving two elements: (a) a confidential relationship existed between the testator and the alleged wrongdoer, and (b) suspicious circumstances surrounded the will's preparation or execution.

**Step 2**: If the contestant proves both elements by a preponderance of the evidence, a presumption of undue influence arises. The presumption means the court will assume undue influence occurred unless the proponent can prove otherwise.

**Step 3**: The burden shifts to the proponent of the will (typically the beneficiary accused of exerting undue influence). The proponent must now prove by **clear and convincing evidence**—a heightened standard—that the will reflects the testator's free, uncoerced wishes. Note that this is a higher burden than the preponderance standard the contestant faced initially.

The practical effect of this burden-shifting is significant. Once the presumption arises, the proponent cannot simply rest on the formal validity of the will. They must affirmatively prove the testator's free will through substantial evidence.

### 4.6 Defeating UI Claims — Defenses

Proponents defending against undue influence claims often rely on the following evidence:

**1. Capacity witnesses**: Witnesses who can testify that the testator understood what they were doing and appeared to be acting of their own free will. However, remember the critical distinction from Romero: capacity and volition are separate inquiries. A testator can have perfect capacity (understanding) while still being unduly influenced (lack of free will). Capacity witnesses help, but they do not automatically defeat an undue influence claim.

**2. Prior relationship justification**: If the beneficiary can show a long history of close relationship with the testator that explains and justifies the disposition, this undermines the "unwarranted benefit" factor. For example, if a testator leaves everything to one child who provided years of care and support, this is less suspicious than leaving everything to a recently hired caregiver.

**3. Public execution**: Saucier held that executing the will at a bank or other public location is a critical factor favoring validity. Public execution "shines the light of day" on the ceremony, making improper influence more difficult and less likely. Independent witnesses from outside the family who observe the execution provide strong evidence of voluntariness.

**4. Independent legal advice**: This is the best defense. If the testator consulted privately with their own attorney, who had no connection to the beneficiary, and the attorney can testify about the testator's clear intent and understanding, this powerfully rebuts any presumption of undue influence. The attorney's testimony that the testator expressed clear, consistent wishes over multiple meetings is particularly valuable.

**5. Testator signed outside beneficiary's presence**: If the beneficiary was not present at the execution ceremony, this suggests they did not exercise last-minute pressure or control. While not dispositive, it helps demonstrate the testator acted independently.

### 4.7 Critical Case

**In re Estate of Saucier**

**Facts**: A healthcare giver received the testator's entire estate under a will. The testator's father, serving as guardian for the testator's minor son, challenged the will on undue influence grounds.

**Holding**: The will was valid. The court found the proponent successfully rebutted the presumption of undue influence.

**Reasoning**: The critical factor was public execution at a bank with independent witnesses. This public ceremony provided powerful evidence that the execution was voluntary and not the product of undue influence. The formality and publicity of the bank setting made it unlikely that the beneficiary had exercised improper coercion. Additional factors supporting validity included independent witnesses who could attest to the testator's understanding and apparent free will.

**Lesson**: When creating estate plans that might be vulnerable to undue influence claims, execute the will publicly with independent witnesses. Consider having the execution occur at a neutral location like a bank, law office, or other professional setting. Include witnesses who have no stake in the outcome and can credibly testify about the testator's demeanor and voluntariness. This creates a strong evidentiary record that can defeat future challenges.

### 4.8 Exam Strategy for UI

When analyzing undue influence issues on an exam, follow this approach:

**1. Identify the type of confidential relationship**: State explicitly whether the relationship is fiduciary, reliant, or dominant-subservient. Explain why the facts support that characterization. If the relationship fits multiple categories, identify all that apply.

**2. Count the suspicious circumstances**: Use the six-factor checklist and evaluate each factor based on the facts. Make this explicit: "The first factor is beneficiary participation in procurement. Here, the facts show..." Go through all six factors systematically. You should identify which factors are present and which are absent.

**3. Apply the burden-shifting framework**: Explain who has the burden at each stage. First, identify what the contestant must prove to create the presumption. Then explain that once the presumption arises, the burden shifts to the proponent to prove by clear and convincing evidence that the will reflects free will. Finally, analyze whether the proponent can meet this burden given the facts.

**4. Distinguish capacity from undue influence**: Remember these are **separate doctrines**. Understanding the terms of a will is not the same as having free will in creating those terms. A testator can have capacity but still be unduly influenced. Make this distinction explicit in your analysis.

**5. Identify the remedy**: If undue influence affects only a specific provision or gift, strike that provision while upholding the remainder of the will. If the undue influence pervades the entire testamentary plan, the entire will fails and the estate passes by intestacy or under a prior valid will.

## §5. FRAUD

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- **Fraud in factum** (deceived about nature of document)?
- **Fraud in inducement** (induced by material misrepresentation)?
- Does fraud affect **specific provision** or **entire will**?
</aside>

### 5.1 Two Types

**Fraud in Factum**: The testator is deceived about the nature of the document they are signing, or the document contains an undisclosed provision. For example, if a testator is told "this is a permission slip for a school field trip" but it is actually a will leaving all property to the person presenting the document, this is fraud in factum. Similarly, if a testator knows they are signing a will but is unaware that the document contains a provision benefiting someone who secretly inserted that language, this too is fraud in factum.

**Fraud in Inducement**: The testator knows they are signing a will and understands its terms, but they were induced to include those terms based on a material misrepresentation of fact. For example, if someone tells a testator "your daughter is dead" when the daughter is actually alive, and the testator therefore leaves everything to their son believing they have no other children, this is fraud in inducement. The testator understood they were making a will and knew what it said, but their intent was formed based on false information.

### 5.2 Remedy

The remedy for fraud depends on its scope. If fraud affects only specific provisions, those provisions are invalidated while the remainder of the will stands. However, if the fraud pervades the entire testamentary plan—meaning the whole will would not have been executed but for the fraud—the entire will may be invalidated. In such cases, the estate would pass either by intestacy or under a prior valid will.

## §6. FORMAL EXECUTION (UPC 2-502)

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist — ATTESTED WILL (UPC 2-502(a))**

- **Writing** present?
- **Signature** by testator OR by another at testator's direction in conscious presence?
- **Two witnesses** signed within reasonable time after witnessing signature/acknowledgment?
- OR **Notarization** instead of witnesses?
- Any interested witnesses? (UPC: doesn't invalidate)
- Signature at end of will?
- If defect exists → consider **harmless error (§9)**
</aside>

### 6.1 Standard Attested Will — UPC 2-502(a)

**UPC 2-502(a)** establishes the formal requirements for executing a valid attested will. Except as provided in subsections (b) and (c) (holographic wills and harmless error), and except as provided in sections 2-503 (harmless error), 2-506, and 2-513, a will must satisfy three core requirements:

**Requirement 1: Writing**

The will must be in writing. Generally, wills statutes require the original writing, not a photocopy. However, if the original is lost or destroyed, a copy may substitute for purposes of probate. Some states allow oral wills (called "nuncupative wills"), but these are rare and subject to strict limitations:

- The will must be made while the testator is in their last illness (some states require the testator to be in extremis, meaning at the point of death)
- The will may pass only personal property under specific dollar limits (typically very low amounts)
- The witnesses must reduce the will to writing shortly after its making, usually within 10 days
- The testator must specifically ask the witnesses to act as witnesses to their statement as a will
- The will must be offered for probate within a limited period after the testator's death

Importantly, an oral will may NOT revoke a prior written will. For exam purposes, you can generally assume written wills are required unless specifically told otherwise.

**Requirement 2: Signature**

**UPC 2-502(a)(2)** requires the will be "signed by the testator or in the testator's name by some other individual in the testator's conscious presence and by the testator's direction."

All wills must be signed by the testator. Any mark applied to the document with the intent that it operate as the testator's signature will fulfill the signature requirement. This could include an "X," initials, a thumbprint, or any other mark, as long as the testator intended it to serve as their signature.

If the testator is unable to sign (due to physical disability, illiteracy, or other reason), another person may sign on behalf of the testator, but this must occur:

- In the testator's **conscious presence** (meaning the testator is aware of what is happening, even if they cannot see it), AND
- At the testator's **direction** (some states require "express direction," meaning the testator must specifically instruct the person to sign on their behalf)

**Requirement 3: Attestation**

**UPC 2-502(a)(3)** provides two alternative methods for attestation:

**(A) Two Witness Signatures**: The will must be signed by at least two individuals, each of whom signed within a reasonable time after the individual witnessed either:

- The signing of the will as described in paragraph (2) (testator's signature or proxy signature), OR
- The testator's acknowledgment of that signature or acknowledgment of the will

This means witnesses need not see the testator actually sign the will; the testator can sign first and then acknowledge to the witnesses that the signature is theirs or that the document is their will. The witnesses must then sign "within a reasonable time" after witnessing or receiving the acknowledgment. What constitutes "reasonable time" is fact-specific, but generally means the same day or within a few days.

**(B) Notarization Alternative**: As an alternative to witness signatures, the testator may acknowledge the will before a notary public or other individual authorized by law to take acknowledgments. This notarization alternative is a more recent innovation, reflecting the trend toward flexibility in will execution formalities. If properly notarized, no witnesses are required.

### 6.2 "Conscious Presence" Test

**Demaris** adopted a liberal approach to the "conscious presence" requirement. The court held that conscious presence means the testator could comprehend the act through any of their senses, not just sight. Line-of-sight is **not required**.

It is sufficient if the witness is in an adjacent room signing the will, as long as the testator is aware of what is happening. The testator need not see the actual signing; they need only be conscious that the witness is performing the act of signing. This liberal interpretation reflects the modern trend away from rigid formalism and toward substantial compliance with the purposes underlying will formalities.

### 6.3 Interested Witnesses

An "interested witness" is someone who receives a benefit under the will they are witnessing. This creates a potential conflict of interest and has historically been viewed with suspicion.

**UPC 2-505(b)** provides: "The signing of a will by an interested party does not invalidate the will or any provision of it." Under the modern UPC approach, the fact that a witness is interested does not affect the validity of the will or the gift to that witness.

**Other jurisdictions** take different approaches:

- Some require "disinterested" witnesses, meaning any gift to an interested witness may invalidate the entire will
- "Purge" statutes allow the will to be valid but purge (eliminate) the gift to the interested witness
- "Lesser of" statutes allow the interested witness to receive the lesser of: (a) the gift under the will, or (b) the share they would have received under intestacy

**Parsons** held that a post-execution disclaimer does not cure the interested witness problem. If a witness was interested at the time of execution, attempting to disclaim or renounce their interest later does not solve the problem. The concern is about improper influence at the moment of execution, and that concern cannot be eliminated by subsequent actions. What matters is the witness's status when they signed the will.

### 6.4 Signature Placement

The general rule is that the testator's signature should be at the end of the will. This prevents someone from adding provisions after the testator signed, ensuring the signature applies to all provisions above it.

Under the **UPC**, words following the signature do not invalidate what precedes the signature. This means if someone adds provisions after the testator signed, those later additions are simply ignored, but the will itself remains valid. Some stricter jurisdictions might invalidate the entire will if material provisions appear after the signature.

### 6.5 Attestation Clause

An attestation clause is a provision at the end of a will, signed by the witnesses, reciting the facts of the execution ceremony. A typical attestation clause might read: "We, the undersigned witnesses, declare that on this date, in our presence, the testator declared this instrument to be their will, signed it voluntarily, and appeared to be of sound mind and under no constraint or undue influence, and we signed as witnesses in the testator's presence and in the presence of each other."

The attestation clause is **not required** for a valid will, but it is extremely helpful evidence. If the will is later challenged, the attestation clause provides a contemporaneous record of what occurred at execution. The clause should state the number of pages in the will to prevent claims that pages were added or removed.

## §7. SELF-PROVING WILLS (UPC 2-504)

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- Simultaneous affidavit (at execution)?
- Post-execution affidavit (attached later)?
- **UPC 2-504(c)**: Signature on affidavit counts as signature on will if needed?
</aside>

### 7.1 Purpose

A self-proving will can be admitted to probate **without live witness testimony**. Normally, when a will is offered for probate, the court requires testimony from the witnesses confirming the testator's signature and that the execution formalities were satisfied. This can be burdensome if witnesses have died, moved away, or cannot remember the execution ceremony. A self-proving affidavit eliminates this burden by creating a contemporaneous sworn record of the execution ceremony that substitutes for later testimony.

### 7.2 Two Methods

**UPC 2-504(a) — Simultaneous Execution and Self-Proving (at time of execution)**:

A will may be simultaneously executed, attested, and made self-proved by acknowledgment by the testator and affidavits of the witnesses. This occurs all at once during the execution ceremony. The testator and witnesses must make their acknowledgments and affidavits before an officer authorized to administer oaths under the laws of the state in which execution occurs (typically a notary public). The officer must provide a certificate under official seal. The statute provides the specific form of language to be used.

This is the most common method. During a single ceremony, the testator signs the will, the witnesses sign as attestors, and then immediately the testator and witnesses sign a self-proving affidavit before a notary, all while together. This creates a comprehensive record of proper execution.

**UPC 2-504(b) — Post-Execution Self-Proving (anytime after execution)**:

An attested will that was not made self-proving at the time of execution may be made self-proving at any time afterward. The testator and witnesses must return, make their acknowledgments and affidavits before an officer authorized to administer oaths (typically a notary), and the officer must attach or annex this self-proving affidavit to the will with a certificate under official seal.

This method provides flexibility for situations where the parties did not create a self-proving affidavit at execution but later realize it would be beneficial. For example, if the testator learns one witness is moving abroad, they might ask both witnesses to return and execute a self-proving affidavit while everyone is still available.

### 7.3 Signature Rule — UPC 2-504(c)

**UPC 2-504(c)** contains a critical provision: "A signature affixed to a self-proving affidavit attached to a will is considered a signature affixed to the will, if necessary to prove the will's due execution."

This means if a witness signed only the self-proving affidavit but forgot to sign the will itself, the signature on the affidavit will be counted as if it appeared on the will. This reflects the substantial compliance doctrine—if the witness performed the essential act of signing in connection with the will, even if they signed in the "wrong place" (the affidavit rather than the will), that signature satisfies the attestation requirement.

**Exam application**: This rule can save a will that would otherwise fail for lack of proper attestation. If you see facts where a witness signed only the affidavit and not the will, invoke UPC 2-504(c) to argue the will is still valid.

## §8. HOLOGRAPHIC WILLS (UPC 2-502(b), (c))

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- **Signature** in testator's handwriting?
- **Material portions** (dispositive language, beneficiaries) in testator's handwriting?
- Can use **extrinsic evidence** (including non-handwritten portions) to prove intent?
- Date present? (Helpful but not required)
- If defect exists → consider **harmless error (§9)**
</aside>

### 8.1 Requirements

Recognizing that in some situations formality is not possible, and that some testators simply believe formal execution is not necessary, about half of the states allow informal, handwritten wills in particular circumstances. Called "holographs," these wills qualify for recognition under an alternative set of rules. Most importantly, holographs need not be witnessed. In return for eliminating the witness requirement, states require additional elements primarily aimed at assuring the genuineness of the document.

**UPC 2-502(b)** establishes three requirements for a valid holographic will:

**1. Signature in the testator's handwriting**: The testator must personally sign the document. This signature serves as authentication that the document is genuine.

**2. Material portions in the testator's handwriting**: The material provisions of the will must be in the testator's handwriting. What constitutes "material" is discussed below.

**3. Testamentary intent**: The document must demonstrate testamentary intent, meaning the testator intended it to function as their will. Under **UPC 2-502(c)**, extrinsic evidence may be used to establish this intent, including portions of the document that are not in the testator's handwriting.

### 8.2 What Are "Material Portions"?

"Material portions" means the substantive testamentary provisions—the dispositive language and identification of beneficiaries. Specifically:

- **Dispositive language**: Words like "I give," "I devise," "I leave," or "I bequeath" followed by descriptions of property
- **Identification of beneficiaries**: Names or descriptions of who should receive property
- **Identification of property**: What is being given (though this may be less critical if the will gives "all my property")

**Not required to be handwritten**:

- Date (though a date is helpful and some states do require it)
- Witness signatures (holographic wills need no witnesses)
- Formal will language (no "last will and testament" necessary)
- Printed letterhead or forms (as long as the material portions are handwritten)

Several states explicitly provide that holographic wills are valid even if "immaterial parts such as date or introductory wording are printed, typed, or stamped" or if printed will forms are used as long as "the material portions of the document are handwritten." This allows testators to use fill-in-the-blank will forms as long as they handwrite the key information.

### 8.3 Extrinsic Evidence — UPC 2-502(c)

**UPC 2-502(c)** provides that intent that the document constitute the testator's will can be established by extrinsic evidence, including, for holographic wills, portions of the document that are not in the testator's handwriting.

This is a significant departure from traditional rules. It means:

- A document with typed letterhead and a handwritten note may qualify as a holographic will
- Non-handwritten portions can be considered to establish testamentary intent, even though they cannot satisfy the "material portions" requirement
- Surrounding circumstances, the testator's statements to others, and other contextual evidence can demonstrate the testator intended the document to be their will

For example, if a testator writes a handwritten letter on printed letterhead saying "I want Sarah to have my house and John to have my bank accounts" and signs it, the handwritten portions satisfy the material-portions requirement, while the letterhead and any extrinsic evidence (like the testator telling friends "this letter is my will") can establish testamentary intent.

### 8.4 Common Patterns

Holographic wills often appear in the following forms:

- **Handwritten letter**: A testator writes a letter to a family member or friend describing how they want their property distributed. If signed and containing material provisions in handwriting, this may be a valid holographic will even if it begins "Dear Sarah" and discusses other matters.
- **Note on existing document**: A testator might handwrite notes on a printed will form, a bank statement, or other document.
- **Holographic codicil on typed will**: A testator with a typed, witnessed will might later handwrite changes on that will and sign next to the changes. If the handwritten portions satisfy holographic will requirements, they may constitute a valid holographic codicil amending the typed will.

### 8.5 Holographic Codicil

Handwritten changes on a typed will can create a holographic codicil if the jurisdiction recognizes holographic wills. The key is that the handwritten changes, if entirely in the testator's handwriting and signed, constitute a separate document (the codicil) that amends the typed will.

If the holographic codicil is valid, it can incorporate the typed will by reference. The doctrine of republication by codicil means the codicil republishes the entire will as of the date of the codicil. This can cure defects in the original will's execution.

**Example**: A typed will lacks proper attestation. Years later, the testator handwrites on the document: "I confirm my will and change the gift to Sarah from $5,000 to $10,000. [Date and signature]." If holographic wills are allowed, this handwritten addition is a valid holographic codicil. It incorporates the typed will by reference and republishes the will as of the codicil's date, potentially curing the original attestation defect.

## §9. HARMLESS ERROR / DISPENSING POWER (UPC 2-503)

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- Is there a **formal defect** in execution (missing witness, unsigned, not holographic, etc.)?
- Is there **clear and convincing evidence** of testamentary intent?
- **Contemporaneous statements** by testator?
- **Formality indicators** (witnesses present even if not compliant)?
- Is this better than **intestacy** for testator's likely intent?
</aside>

### 9.1 Rule

**UPC 2-503** establishes the harmless error doctrine (also called the dispensing power). It provides:

"Although a document or writing added upon a document was not executed in compliance with Section 2-502, the document or writing is treated as if it had been executed in compliance with that section if the proponent of the document or writing establishes by clear and convincing evidence that the decedent intended the document or writing to constitute:

(i) the decedent's will,

(ii) a partial or complete revocation of the will,

(iii) an addition to or an alteration of the will, or

(iv) a partial or complete revival of his or her formerly revoked will or of a formerly revoked portion of the will."

In essence, a court may dispense with the execution formalities if satisfied by clear and convincing evidence that the decedent intended the document to be their will (or codicil, revocation, or revival). This shifts the focus from whether the statutory formalities were satisfied to whether the testator actually intended the document to have testamentary effect.

The harmless error doctrine represents a fundamental challenge to formalism in wills law. Traditionally, strict compliance with every statutory formality was required, no matter how trivial the defect or how clear the testator's intent. The UPC's harmless error provision reflects the modern view that testamentary intent should triumph over technical failures when intent can be proven by clear and convincing evidence.

### 9.2 Standard: Clear and Convincing Evidence

Clear and convincing evidence is a higher standard than preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not) but lower than beyond a reasonable doubt. It requires that the evidence make the proposition highly probable. The proponent must present enough evidence that the court has a firm conviction that the testator intended the document to be their will.

What constitutes clear and convincing evidence of testamentary intent? Courts look for:

**Contemporaneous statements by the testator**: Did the testator tell others, around the time of creating the document, that it was their will? These statements are powerful evidence. For example, if the testator gave a letter to a friend saying "This contains my wishes for after I die" or "This is my will," such statements strongly support testamentary intent.

**Formality indicators**: Did the testator attempt to comply with formalities, even if unsuccessfully? The presence of witnesses (even if insufficient number or improperly done), a signature, a date, language like "this is my last will," or other attempts at formality suggest the testator understood they were creating a legally operative document.

**Content**: Does the document use dispositive language and make a comprehensive disposition of property? Language like "I give," "I devise," or "I leave" combined with specific bequests suggests testamentary intent. A document that disposes of all or substantially all of the testator's property looks more like a will than a mere statement of wishes.

**Circumstances**: Why did the testator create the document? Was it created when the testator was facing death or anticipated death? Was it carefully kept in a safe location with other important papers? These circumstances can suggest testamentary intent.

### 9.3 Critical Case

**In re Estate of Wiltfong**

**Facts**: In the presence of friends, the decedent gave the proponent a birthday card containing a typed letter the decedent had signed. The letter expressed the decedent's wish that if anything should ever happen to him, everything he owned should go to the proponent. The letter also stated that the proponent, their pets, and an aunt were his only family, and "everyone else is dead to me." The decedent told the proponent and the friends present that the letter represented his wishes. The trial court ruled it was not a will because it did not meet Colorado's holographic will requirements (material portions must be in the testator's handwriting, but this letter was typed).

**Issue**: Can a typed letter qualify as a will under the harmless error doctrine when it fails to meet holographic will requirements?

**Holding**: Yes. The court admitted the typed letter as a will under the harmless error provision.

**Reasoning**: Colorado law normally requires that for a holographic will, the signature and material portions must be in the testator's handwriting. This letter failed that requirement because it was typed. However, under a proper formulation of harmless error analysis, once a court determines a decedent signed or acknowledged a document as a will (as occurred here), the issue becomes whether the proponent can establish by clear and convincing evidence the decedent intended the document to be a will.

This proof may take the form of extrinsic evidence, such as the decedent's statements to others about the letter, the language of the letter itself, and surrounding circumstances. Here:

1. **Decedent's statements**: The decedent explicitly told the proponent and friends that the letter represented his wishes about what should happen to his property after his death.
2. **Language of the letter**: The letter used terms like "inherit," which has inherently testamentary meaning. The letter comprehensively disposed of "everything he owned."
3. **Formality**: The letter was signed by the decedent and also bore the signatures of two witnesses (the friends who were present), even though they did not attest in the formal sense. The fact that the decedent had witnesses sign demonstrates his understanding that some formality was appropriate for a will.
4. **Circumstances**: The letter was given to the intended beneficiary in a ceremonial manner (on a birthday) in the presence of others, suggesting its significance.

Together, this evidence clearly and convincingly established that the decedent intended the typed letter to be his will, despite the failure to comply with holographic will formalities.

**Lesson**: Harmless error can save documents that would fail under traditional rules. The doctrine focuses on testamentary intent rather than technical compliance. When analyzing harmless error issues, gather all evidence of the testator's intent—their statements, the formality indicators in the document, and the surrounding circumstances.

### 9.4 Exam Application

**When to argue UPC 2-503**:

1. **Identify a formal defect**: First, determine what formal requirement has not been satisfied. Missing a witness? No signature? Typed rather than handwritten? Failed notarization? Identify the specific failure.
2. **Assess testamentary intent evidence**: Look for evidence that demonstrates the testator intended the document to be their will. This is your path to invoking harmless error.
3. **Consider alternatives**: Is harmless error better than the alternative (intestacy or a prior will)? If the defective will's disposition is much closer to what the testator likely wanted than the alternative, this supports applying harmless error.

**What harmless error CANNOT do**:

Harmless error cannot create new substantive provisions or change what the testator wrote. It can only excuse formal defects in execution. If the will is ambiguous about who gets what, harmless error cannot resolve that ambiguity by looking at what the testator "probably meant." Harmless error applies only to execution formalities, not to substantive interpretation.

**Strategy**: In any exam problem where a will has an execution defect, you should:

1. First analyze whether the will satisfies the traditional execution requirements
2. If not, identify the specific defect
3. Then analyze whether harmless error could excuse that defect
4. Assess the strength of evidence of testamentary intent
5. Apply the clear and convincing standard to determine whether the doctrine would likely apply

## §10. CHOICE OF LAW (UPC 2-502)

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist — Valid if complies with law of ANY:**

- Place of **execution**?
- Testator's **domicile** at execution or death?
- Testator's **place of abode** at execution/death?
- Testator's **nationality** at execution/death?
</aside>

To encourage will creation and avoid invalidating wills based on technical choice-of-law issues, the UPC provides multiple safe harbors. A written will is valid if its execution complies with the law at the time of execution of **ANY** of the following:

1. The place where the will is **executed**, OR
2. The testator's **domicile at execution**, OR
3. The testator's **domicile at death**, OR
4. The testator's **place of abode at execution or death**, OR
5. The testator's **nationality at execution or death**

The practical effect is that a will validly executed under any reasonable jurisdiction's law will be recognized. For example, if a Pennsylvania resident executes a will while vacationing in Florida, the will is valid if it complies with either Pennsylvania law, Florida law, or potentially the law of any other state or country with which the testator has relevant contacts.

**Exam tip**: Multiple safe harbors make validation easy. If you're analyzing validity and the will fails under one state's law, check whether it might satisfy another state's law. Given the multiple choice-of-law options, most wills that show testamentary intent will satisfy some jurisdiction's requirements.

## §11. REVOCATION

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

**Subsequent Writing Method:**

- **Express revocation** ("I revoke all prior wills")?
- **Implied revocation by inconsistency** (new will complete vs. incomplete disposition)?

**Physical Act Method (UPC 2-507(a)(2)):**

- Act of **burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating, destroying**?
- Act performed **on the original** (not copy)?
- **Intent to revoke**?
- By testator OR by another in testator's **conscious presence** and at testator's **direction**?
- **Lost will presumption** (will last in testator's possession but cannot be found)?

**If new will failed:**

- Consider **DRR** (§13)
- Consider **Revival** (§12)

</callopt>

As times change, estate plans may also need to change. The law has established specific methods for revoking or amending wills. Moreover, if the testator does not act, the law may revoke a will in situations where the legislature thinks the typical testator would have wanted that result. Testators can modify or revoke their wills by either of two basic methods: (1) a writing that meets statutory requirements, or (2) specific physical acts that damage the will.

### 11.1 Two Methods (UPC 2-507)

**UPC 2-507(a)** provides: "A will or any part thereof is revoked:"

### Method 1: Subsequent Writing

A subsequent instrument can revoke a prior will in two ways:

**1. Express revocation**: **UPC 2-507(a)(1)** allows revocation "by executing a subsequent will that revokes the previous will or part expressly." The clearest form is a clause stating "I hereby revoke all prior wills and codicils." This unambiguously establishes that the new will completely replaces anything that came before.

The revoking document must itself satisfy will-execution requirements. In a jurisdiction that allows holographic wills, a short handwritten note saying "I revoke my will dated [date]" and signed by the testator may effectively revoke a long, formal, typed will. This is because the holographic revocation is itself a valid testamentary instrument.

**2. Implied revocation by inconsistency**: **UPC 2-507(a)(1)** also allows revocation "by inconsistency." If a subsequent will contradicts a prior will, the subsequent will revokes the prior will to the extent of the inconsistency.

**UPC 2-507(b), (c), and (d)** establish presumptions about revocation by inconsistency:

**UPC 2-507(c)**: If a subsequent will makes a **complete disposition** of the testator's estate (meaning it disposes of all or substantially all property), the testator is **presumed** to have intended the subsequent will to replace rather than supplement the previous will. If this presumption applies and is not rebutted by clear and convincing evidence, the previous will is completely revoked; only the subsequent will is operative.

**UPC 2-507(d)**: If a subsequent will does **not** make a complete disposition of the testator's estate (meaning it disposes of only some property), the testator is **presumed** to have intended the subsequent will to supplement rather than replace the previous will. If this presumption applies and is not rebutted by clear and convincing evidence, the subsequent will revokes the previous will only to the extent the two are inconsistent; each will is fully operative to the extent they are not inconsistent.

These are rebuttable presumptions. Clear and convincing evidence can overcome them.

**Example of complete disposition**: Will II states "I give all my property to Sarah." This is a complete disposition. The presumption is that Will II completely revokes Will I.

**Example of incomplete disposition**: Will II states "I give my car to Sarah." This disposes of only one asset. The presumption is that Will II supplements Will I, revoking only any prior provision about the car but leaving the rest of Will I intact.

### Method 2: Physical Act (UPC 2-507(a)(2))

**UPC 2-507(a)(2)** allows revocation "by performing a revocatory act on the will, if the testator performed the act with the intent and for the purpose of revoking the will or part or if another individual performed the act in the testator's conscious presence and by the testator's direction."

**Four requirements must be satisfied:**

**1. Revocatory Act**: The statute defines "revocatory act on the will" to include "burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating, or destroying the will or any part of it." Each of these terms has specific meaning:

- **Burning**: Setting the will on fire and allowing it to burn, even partially
- **Tearing**: Ripping the will, even if only slightly torn
- **Canceling**: Writing on the will with revocatory intent (such as writing "VOID," "REVOKED," or crossing out provisions)
- **Obliterating**: Making the text illegible, such as by marking through it heavily
- **Destroying**: Any other act of physical destruction

Critically, the statute clarifies: "A burning, tearing, or canceling is a 'revocatory act on the will,' whether or not the burn, tear, or cancellation touched any of the words on the will." This means even if you burn the edge of the will without burning any text, it counts as a revocatory act as long as you intended to revoke.

**2. On the Will**: The physical act must be performed **on the original will**, not on a copy. This is one of the most important limitations. Acts performed on photocopies, scanned copies, or photographs of the will do not revoke the original. (Estate of Gushwa)

**3. Intent to Revoke**: The testator must perform the act with the intent and for the purpose of revoking the will. Accidental damage does not revoke a will. If a will is accidentally torn or burned, it is not revoked because the requisite intent was absent.

**4. By Testator or Proxy**: Either the testator must personally perform the act, OR another individual must perform the act "in the testator's conscious presence and by the testator's direction."

"Conscious presence" has the same liberal meaning here as in the execution context. The testator need not see the act; they need only be aware it is happening.

"Direction" means the testator must specifically instruct the other person to perform the revocatory act. General authorization is insufficient; the testator must direct the specific act of revocation.

### 11.2 Physical Act — Key Rules

**"Canceling" — Writing on the Will**

Writing on a will with revocatory intent constitutes canceling. Common examples include writing "VOID," "REVOKED," "NULL," or drawing large X marks through provisions or across pages. The key is revocatory intent; merely correcting a typo or making a note does not revoke.

**Partial vs. Complete Revocation**

Physical acts can revoke an entire will or only part of it:

- **Crossing out a specific provision** may revoke only that provision while leaving the rest of the will intact
- **Removing the signature** likely revokes the entire will because the signature is the most important part (In re Estate of Bakhaus)
- **Burning or destroying the entire document** clearly revokes the whole will
- **Writing "VOID" across multiple pages** may revoke the whole will if the intent was to revoke everything

**Lost Will Presumption**

If a will was last in the testator's possession and control but cannot be found after death, the law **presumes** it was revoked by physical act. This is a rebuttable presumption. The reasoning is that if the testator had the will and it's now missing, the most likely explanation is that the testator destroyed it with intent to revoke.

To rebut this presumption, proponents must present evidence explaining the will's absence. For example:

- Evidence that someone else had access to the will and might have destroyed it
- Evidence of suspicious circumstances around the will's disappearance
- Evidence that the testator carefully kept the will and would not have destroyed it

If the presumption is not rebutted, the will is treated as revoked and the estate passes by intestacy or under a prior will.

**Copy Rule — Original Required**

A physical act on a **copy** does NOT revoke the original. (Estate of Gushwa) This rule is absolute. No matter how clear the testator's intent, if they perform a revocatory act on a photocopy while the original remains intact elsewhere, the will is not revoked.

This can create problems when the testator does not have access to the original (for example, if an attorney or other person holds it). In such cases, the testator should execute a subsequent will expressly revoking the prior will, rather than attempting physical act revocation on a copy.

### 11.3 Critical Cases

**In re Estate of Bakhaus**

**Rule**: Removing the signature from a will revokes the entire will because the signature is the most important part of the will.

**Facts and Analysis**: Cases involving tearing or cutting a will fall into two categories. First, cases where certain provisions or portions are removed by cutting or tearing. Second, cases where the signature is removed or obliterated.

In the first category, courts have held that drawing a line through or cutting out certain portions is an attempt to revoke only those portions. In the absence of evidence of intent to revoke the entire will, such partial revocation is disregarded (in jurisdictions not allowing partial revocation) and the will is admitted to probate as it originally existed, so far as legible.

In the second category, courts have held that where the testator's signature has been cut from the will or codicil, the will or codicil is completely revoked. The signature is the most important part of the will. If one unfamiliar with statute law were to revoke a will, cutting out the signature would be an ordinary mode to adopt, since cutting signatures from documents is frequently used to indicate their cancellation.

**Additional principle**: When a will is found in the testator's possession and control in a mutilated condition, the law presumes the mutilation was done by the testator with the requisite intent to revoke. The burden shifts to proponents to rebut this presumption.

**Lesson**: Where the testator makes physical changes matters enormously. Crossing out a provision may revoke only that provision. But cutting out or defacing the signature revokes the entire will.

---

**Estate of Gushwa**

**Rule**: A physical act must be performed on the original will. An act on a photocopy is insufficient to revoke the will.

**Facts**: George Gushwa executed a will in 2000. Ted Dale possessed the original will. Gushwa apparently wished to revoke the will. Gushwa's attorney drafted a document titled "Revocation of Missing Will(s)." Ted first sent Gushwa three photocopied pages from the will. Gushwa wrote "Revoked" on each of the three pages. Later, Gushwa obtained a photocopy of the entire will and wrote "Revoked" on each page. After Gushwa's death, litigation ensued over whether the will had been validly revoked.

**Holding**: The will was NOT revoked. Writing "Revoked" on a photocopy does not constitute a valid physical act revocation. The statute requires that the act be performed on the original will. Acts on photocopies are insufficient, no matter how clear the intent.

**What Gushwa should have done**: He should have executed a subsequent will (which could be a simple holographic will if the jurisdiction allows them). He could have written entirely in his own handwriting: "I hereby revoke all prior wills. [Date and signature]." This would have been a valid holographic will expressly revoking the prior will, accomplishing his goal.

**Lesson**: When the original is not accessible, use revocation by subsequent instrument rather than attempting physical act revocation. The original-document requirement for physical acts is strict and cannot be avoided.

### 11.4 Revocation by Operation of Law

In addition to intentional revocation by the testator, certain life events may automatically revoke parts of a will by operation of law:

**Divorce**: Many states have statutes providing that divorce revokes any provisions benefiting the former spouse. Typically, the will is read as if the former spouse predeceased the testator. This prevents an ex-spouse from inheriting when the testator likely would have changed the will but died before doing so.

**Marriage**: Some states give a surviving spouse who married the testator after will execution an intestate share, essentially carving out a portion of the estate for the new spouse.

**Birth of child**: Pretermitted heir statutes in many states give a share to children born after will execution who are not mentioned in the will, on the assumption the testator would have included the child but died before updating the will.

These statutory revocations reflect legislative judgments about what testators would typically want. However, well-drafted wills often include provisions addressing these contingencies (such as "if I marry, this will shall remain in full effect" or "I intentionally make no provision for any children born after the date of this will").

## §12. REVIVAL (UPC 2-509)

**Scenario**: Will I → Will II (revokes Will I) → Will II is revoked → Is Will I back in effect?

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- How was Will II revoked? **(Physical act vs. subsequent writing = different rules)**
- **Physical act revocation** (§2-509(a)): Will I remains revoked unless **evidence** shows intent to revive (extrinsic evidence OK)
- **Partial physical act** (§2-509(b)): **Presumption** Will I revived (opponent must rebut)
- **Subsequent writing revocation** (§2-509(c)): Will I remains revoked unless **four corners of Will III** show intent to revive (NO extrinsic evidence)
</aside>

Revival addresses what happens to a prior will when a subsequent will that revoked it is itself revoked. The issue arises in this sequence: Testator executes Will I, then executes Will II which revokes Will I, then revokes Will II. What is the status of Will I?

### 12.1 Three Approaches

Jurisdictions historically took three different approaches:

**English/Uncovered Rule**: Will I is automatically revived when Will II is revoked. Because wills "speak" only at death, Will I was never truly revoked—it was merely "covered up" by Will II. When Will II is destroyed, Will I is "uncovered" and becomes operative again.

**Reborn Approach**: Will I was completely revoked by Will II and remains revoked unless the testator re-executes it. To revive Will I, the testator must reproduce the old will and execute a new, clean copy with all formalities. Will I must be "reborn" through fresh execution.

**UPC/Revival Approach**: Whether Will I is revived depends on the testator's intent. The UPC provides specific rules that vary based on how Will II was revoked and whether the revocation was complete or partial.

### 12.2 UPC 2-509 Rules

**UPC 2-509** establishes different rules depending on the method and scope of revocation:

### (a) Subsequent Will Wholly Revoked by Physical Act

**UPC 2-509(a)**: "If a subsequent will that wholly revoked a previous will is thereafter revoked by a revocatory act under Section 2-507(a)(2), the previous will **remains revoked UNLESS it is revived**. The previous will is revived if it is evident from the circumstances of the revocation of the subsequent will or from the testator's contemporary or subsequent declarations that the testator intended the previous will to take effect as executed."

**Application**: Will I → Will II (wholly revokes Will I) → Testator physically destroys Will II

**Default rule**: Will I remains revoked

**Revival exception**: Will I is revived if evidence (including extrinsic evidence) shows the testator intended to revive Will I

**Key points**:

- The burden is on the party seeking revival to prove the testator's intent to revive
- Extrinsic evidence is admissible, including the circumstances of revocation and the testator's statements
- Look at: how Will II was destroyed, what the testator said when destroying it, the testator's subsequent statements about their intentions

**Example**: Testator tears up Will II while saying to witnesses, "I want my old will back in effect." This statement is strong evidence of intent to revive Will I.

**Counter-example**: Testator tears up Will II while saying, "I don't want any will." This suggests intent to die intestate, not to revive Will I.

### (b) Subsequent Will Partly Revoked by Physical Act

**UPC 2-509(b)**: "If a subsequent will that partly revoked a previous will is thereafter revoked by a revocatory act under Section 2-507(a)(2), a revoked part of the previous will is revived **unless it is evident** from the circumstances of the revocation of the subsequent will or from the testator's contemporary or subsequent declarations that the testator did not intend the revoked part to take effect as executed."

**Application**: Will I → Will II (partly revokes Will I, changing one provision) → Testator physically destroys Will II

**Default rule**: The revoked part of Will I **is presumed revived**

**Exception**: The revoked part remains revoked if evidence shows the testator did NOT intend revival

**Key points**:

- This presumes revival, unlike subsection (a) which presumes no revival
- The burden shifts: the opponent of revival must prove the testator did NOT intend revival
- The logic is that if Will II only made minor changes and the testator destroys Will II, they likely want to return to the original plan in Will I

**Example**: Will I leaves $50,000 to Sarah. Will II changes this to $25,000 to Sarah. Testator tears up Will II. Presumption: The $50,000 gift in Will I is revived. Opponent would need evidence that testator wanted Sarah to get nothing, not $50,000.

### (c) Subsequent Will Revoked by Another Subsequent Will

**UPC 2-509(c)**: "If a subsequent will that revoked a previous will in whole or in part is thereafter revoked by another, later will, the previous will remains revoked in whole or in part, **unless it or its revoked part is revived**. The previous will or its revoked part is revived **to the extent it appears from the terms of the later will** that the testator intended the previous will to take effect."

**Application**: Will I → Will II (revokes Will I) → Will III (revokes Will II)

**Default rule**: Will I remains revoked

**Revival exception**: Will I is revived **only if the four corners of Will III** show that intent

**Key points**:

- **No extrinsic evidence** is admissible under this subsection
- Revival can be established only from "the terms of the later will" (Will III)
- The testator must express revival intent in Will III itself

**Why the difference?**: When revocation occurs by another written instrument (Will III), the law presumes the testator had the opportunity to express their intent in that written instrument. Therefore, only the written instrument itself is considered.

**Example**: Will III states: "I revoke my will dated [Will II date] and I hereby reinstate and republish my will dated [Will I date]." This clearly revives Will I.

**Counter-example**: Will III states: "I revoke my will dated [Will II date]." Without more, this does not revive Will I. Even if the testator told friends "I want Will I back," that extrinsic evidence is inadmissible under subsection (c).

### 12.3 Exam Approach

When analyzing revival issues, follow these steps:

**1. Identify the sequence of wills**: Map out Will I, Will II, and potentially Will III.

**2. Identify how the subsequent will was revoked**: Was it revoked by physical act or by another will? This determines which subsection of 2-509 applies.

**3. Apply the correct subsection**:

- Physical act + whole revocation = 2-509(a): no revival unless evidence shows intent to revive
- Physical act + partial revocation = 2-509(b): presumption of revival unless evidence shows no intent to revive
- Subsequent instrument = 2-509(c): no revival unless four corners of later will show intent to revive

**4. Note burden and evidence rules**:

- Subsections (a) and (b): extrinsic evidence admissible
- Subsection (c): only four corners of the later will; no extrinsic evidence

**5. Assess whether the presumption is rebutted or the exception established**: Look at the facts to determine whether there's sufficient evidence to apply the revival exception or rebut the revival presumption.

**Key distinction**: The most important difference is physical act (subsections a and b) versus subsequent instrument (subsection c). With physical acts, courts can consider extrinsic evidence of the testator's intent. With subsequent instruments, courts look only at the language of the later will itself.

## §13. DEPENDENT RELATIVE REVOCATION (DRR)

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist — "Law of Second Best"**

- Did testator **revoke** old will?
- Did **new will fail**?
- Are old and new wills **substantially similar** (same dispositive plan)?
- **Clear and convincing evidence** that revocation was **conditional** on new will's validity?
- Is restoring old will **closer to testator's intent** than intestacy?

**If YES to all** → Apply DRR to restore old will

</aside>

### 13.1 Concept

Dependent Relative Revocation (DRR) is known to law students as the "undo button" for will revocations. It treats a revocation as ineffective if the revocation was based on a mistaken assumption of law or a false belief about fact.

The classic scenario: A testator destroys Will I believing Will II is valid. Will II turns out to be invalid (due to defective execution, lack of capacity, etc.). Did the destruction of Will I effectively revoke it? DRR says no—the revocation of Will I was dependent on and relative to the mistaken belief that Will II was valid. If the court can determine the testator would not have revoked Will I had they known Will II was invalid, DRR treats the revocation as ineffective.

DRR does not give the testator what they really wanted (the new Will II). Rather, when the choice is between intestacy and the old will (Will I), DRR restores the old will as the "second best" option that better approximates the testator's intent.

### 13.2 When DRR Applies

Under the **Third Restatement of Property**, a revocation is presumed ineffective if:

1. The revocation was made because of a **false assumption of law** OR a **false belief about an objective fact**, AND
2. Allowing the revocation to remain effective would be **inconsistent** with the testator's probable intent

**Common scenario**: Testator destroys Will I thinking Will II is valid; Will II fails. The false assumption is that Will II is valid. Allowing the revocation to stand would mean intestacy, which is inconsistent with the testator's intent to have an estate plan.

### 13.3 Requirements (Third Restatement)

To apply DRR, three elements must be satisfied:

**1. Clear intent that revocation was conditional**: There must be clear evidence that the testator's revocation of the old will was conditional on the validity or effectiveness of the new will. The testator must have thought, essentially, "I'm revoking the old will because the new will is going to replace it."

**2. Substantial similarity**: The new will must be substantially similar to the old will, showing the same basic dispositive plan. If the wills make dramatically different dispositions, DRR should not apply because restoring the old will would not approximate what the testator wanted.

**3. Clear and convincing evidence**: The conditional intent must be proved by clear and convincing evidence, not speculation or conjecture.

### 13.4 Critical Case

**In re Estate of Patten**

**Facts**: Ella executed two wills—a valid will in 1968 and another in 1970. The 1970 will was invalid. Ella had destroyed the 1968 will. After Ella died, the question was whether DRR could restore the 1968 will.

**Issue**: Can DRR apply to restore a destroyed will when the testator executed an invalid replacement will?

**Holding**: No DRR. The 1968 will was not restored.

**Reasoning**:

First, the court emphasized that **DRR is to be applied with caution**. It is a doctrine that should be narrowly applied, not broadly invoked whenever a testator makes a mistake.

Second, for DRR to apply, the **new will must not have changed the testamentary purpose** of the old will and must essentially repeat the same dispositive plan such that it is clear the first will was revoked only because the second duplicated its purpose. The dispositive plan must be substantially the same.

Third, the testator's "intent" is the controlling factor, but evidence of this intent **cannot be left to speculation, supposition, conjecture, or possibility**. A showing of immediate intent to make a new will and of conditional destruction are required to reestablish a destroyed will under DRR.

Here, while the content of both wills was similar, the **dissimilarities were such that they revealed the decedent's revocation of the 1968 will was not conditioned on the validity of the 1970 will**. The differences in the wills showed that the decedent may not have intended the same dispositive plan. The evidence that the decedent intended the revocation to be conditional on Will II's validity was merely conjecture and speculation. DRR can only be applied where the evidence of the testator's intent is clear and convincing.

**Lesson**: DRR is available, but it is not easy to establish. Courts require clear and convincing evidence of conditional intent and substantial similarity between the wills. If there are significant differences between the old and new wills, courts will not apply DRR because they cannot confidently say the old will approximates what the testator wanted.

### 13.5 Exam Strategy

When analyzing DRR issues, think of it as "the law of second best." Follow this framework:

**"Law of Second Best" Analysis**:

1. **Did the testator revoke the old will?** If yes, proceed.
2. **Did the new will fail?** If yes, proceed. (If the new will is valid, there's no need for DRR—the new will governs.)
3. **Are the old and new wills substantially similar?** Compare the dispositive plans. Do they leave property to the same people in similar proportions? If the plans are dramatically different, DRR likely will not apply.
4. **Is there evidence the revocation was conditional?** Look for facts showing the testator revoked the old will because they thought the new will would be effective. Did the testator execute the old and new wills at the same time? Did the testator say anything suggesting they were replacing one with the other?
5. **Is restoring the old will closer to the testator's intent than intestacy?** DRR is about choosing the lesser of two evils. If the old will gives most property to Sarah and the failed new will gives most property to Sarah, then intestacy (which might give everything to the testator's children) is inconsistent with the testator's apparent intent. Restoring the old will better approximates what the testator wanted.

**If the answer to questions 3-5 is yes**, apply DRR to restore the old will.

**If any answer is no**, DRR does not apply and the estate passes by intestacy (or under an earlier will if one exists).

**Common mistakes to avoid**:

- Don't apply DRR just because it seems "fair" or because intestacy seems like a bad outcome
- DRR requires substantial similarity—not identity, but close resemblance
- Remember the burden: clear and convincing evidence, not just "it seems likely"

## §14. INCORPORATION BY REFERENCE

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- **Writing in existence** at will execution (or at codicil execution if republication)?
- Will describes writing with **sufficient detail** to identify it?
- Will **intends to incorporate** the writing?
- Any **republication by codicil** that brings documents into existence window?
</aside>

Incorporation by reference is a way to give testamentary effect to a document that is not present at the will's execution or that was not executed with testamentary formalities. It allows a will to "incorporate" another document by referring to it, making that document part of the will.

### 14.1 Requirements (Common Law)

At common law, four elements must be satisfied for a document to be incorporated by reference:

**1. Writing in existence at will execution**: The document being incorporated must have existed at the time the will was executed. You cannot incorporate a document that doesn't yet exist.

**2. Will describes writing as in existence**: The will must describe the document as currently existing (not as something to be created in the future).

**3. Sufficient detail to identify the writing**: The will must describe the document with enough detail that it can be identified. This might include describing the type of document, its date, where it is kept, or other identifying details.

**4. Intent to incorporate**: The will must manifest an intent to incorporate the document—to make it part of the testamentary plan.

### 14.2 UPC 2-510

**UPC 2-510** eliminates the second element (that the will must describe the writing as being "in existence"). Under the UPC, you need not use language stating the document "exists" or is "in existence"; you simply need to describe it with sufficient detail and demonstrate intent to incorporate it.

This is a relaxation of the common law rule, making incorporation easier.

### 14.3 Republication by Codicil

**Republication by codicil** is a critical doctrine that expands incorporation by reference. When a testator executes a codicil, the codicil **republishes the will as of the date of the codicil**. The legal effect is as if the will were re-executed on the date of the codicil.

This has a profound effect on incorporation by reference: **documents created after the original will but before the codicil can be incorporated**, because the will is treated as having been executed on the codicil date (when those documents were in existence).

How it works

:

**Example**: Testator executes Will I on January 1, 2020. The will states: "I incorporate by reference the letter dated December 1, 2019, which I keep in my desk drawer." This works—the letter existed before the will.

Now suppose on June 1, 2020, the testator creates a new letter with different instructions and wants to incorporate it. The testator cannot amend Will I to incorporate the June 2020 letter because that letter did not exist when Will I was executed.

However, if the testator executes a holographic codicil on July 1, 2020, stating: "I incorporate by reference the letter dated June 1, 2020," the codicil republishes Will I as of July 1, 2020. Since the June 2020 letter existed on July 1, 2020, it can be incorporated.

**Curing Execution Defects**

Republication by codicil can also cure defects in the original will's execution:

- **Missing signature**: If Will I lacks the testator's signature, but a valid codicil refers to and republishes Will I, the codicil cures the signature defect.
- **Missing witnesses**: If Will I lacks proper attestation, a holographic codicil (which needs no witnesses) can republish Will I, curing the attestation defect.
- **Date issues**: If Will I is undated, the codicil provides a date of republication.

### 14.4 Holographic Codicil Strategy

A powerful estate planning technique is to use a holographic codicil to cure defects in a typed will or to incorporate documents by reference. This strategy requires:

1. The jurisdiction must recognize holographic wills
2. The testator creates an entirely handwritten, signed, and dated document
3. The holographic document explicitly refers to the typed will and either confirms it or makes changes

**Example**: A typed will lacking proper witness signatures can be validated if the testator later handwrites: "This writing confirms and republishes my will dated [date], and I change the gift to Sarah from $5,000 to $10,000. Signed [signature] Dated [date]."

Because the holographic codicil is valid (assuming material portions in handwriting, signed, and showing testamentary intent), it republishes the typed will as of the codicil date, curing the attestation defect in the original will.

### 14.5 Critical Case

**Simon v. Grayson**

**Rule**: Republication by codicil satisfies the "existing document" requirement for incorporation by reference.

**Facts**: A testator's will provided $6,000 to executors with instructions that a letter with distribution instructions would be found in his personal effects, dated March 25, 1932. A letter was found, but it was dated July 3, 1933—after the will's execution.

**Holding**: Although the letter did not exist when the will was executed, a later republication of the will by codicil satisfied the "existing document" rule and incorporated the letter by reference, provided the testamentary instruments sufficiently identify it.

**Lesson**: If you want to incorporate a document that was created after your will, execute a codicil. The codicil republishes the will as of the codicil's date, bringing the document into the "existing at time of execution" window.

**Practical application**: This doctrine allows testators significant flexibility. Rather than executing an entirely new will, they can execute a simple codicil (potentially holographic) that republishes the will and incorporates documents created since the will's original execution.

## §15. INDEPENDENT SIGNIFICANCE

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- Are acts **outside the will** given testamentary effect?
- Do those acts have **significance independent of the will** (performed for non-testamentary life reasons)?
- Did testator perform act **only** to affect will disposition, OR for independent life reasons?
</aside>

### 15.1 Doctrine

The doctrine of independent significance allows wills to reference information outside the will and give testamentary effect to acts performed outside the will, as long as those acts have significance independent of their effect on the will.

Courts regularly give testamentary effect to information outside a will when identifying people and property. The key distinction is whether the external acts or facts have significance beyond their testamentary effect. Acts of independent significance don't have to comply with will formalities because they are not primarily testamentary in nature.

### 15.2 Classic Examples

**Valid (acts have independent significance):**

- **"My wife gets my stocks"**: The identity of "my wife" is determined by marriage, not by the will. The testator marries for reasons having nothing to do with the will (love, companionship, etc.). Marriage is an act of independent significance.
- **"Contents of my safe deposit box to X"**: The testator places items in the safe deposit box for non-testamentary reasons—safekeeping, organization, convenience. The contents change over time as the testator adds or removes items for practical life reasons, not primarily to change what X receives under the will.
- **"Furniture in my den to my son"**: The testator arranges and rearranges furniture for living purposes—comfort, aesthetics, functionality. Moving furniture into or out of the den happens for reasons independent of the will's operation.
- **"The car I own at my death to my daughter"**: The testator buys and sells cars throughout life for transportation needs, not to change what the daughter inherits. Trading in an old car for a new one is an act of independent significance because it serves the testator's current transportation needs.

**Invalid (acts have purely testamentary significance):**

- **"Letters in my safe deposit box designating beneficiaries"**: If the testator places letters in the safe deposit box solely to designate who receives property at death, those letters serve no purpose except a testamentary one. They must comply with will formalities.
- **"Items I mark with a red sticker go to Sarah"**: If the testator has no reason to mark items with red stickers except to designate what Sarah should receive, the marking is purely testamentary and must comply with will formalities.

### 15.3 Test

Ask: Did the testator perform the act **only** to affect the will's disposition, OR for independent life reasons?

If the act serves life purposes beyond its testamentary effect, the doctrine of independent significance applies and the act need not comply with will formalities. The act changes the will's practical effect, but because the testator performs the act for non-testamentary reasons, the law does not require testamentary formalities.

**Grey area example**: "Items I place in the locked cabinet in my study go to John." This could go either way. If the testator uses the locked cabinet to store valuable items for safekeeping during life, it has independent significance. If the testator creates the locked cabinet solely as a method of designating what John should inherit, it lacks independent significance.

The doctrine recognizes that life goes on after will execution. People marry, divorce, acquire and dispose of property, move belongings, and make countless decisions for reasons having nothing to do with their estate plan. The law allows wills to incorporate these life changes without requiring the testator to execute a new will every time circumstances change.

## §16. CONTRACTS CONCERNING WILLS

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- Is there a **contract to make/not revoke** a will?
- Are these **mutual wills** (two separate instruments with reciprocal provisions)?
- Is this a **joint will** (single instrument with 2+ wills)?
- Presumption: Mutual wills with reciprocal provisions → **presumed contractual**
</aside>

### 16.1 Types

People can execute contracts concerning wills. These contracts may take several forms:

- **Contract to make a will**: A person promises to execute a will with specific provisions
- **Contract not to revoke a will**: A person promises not to revoke an existing will
- **Contract concerning mutual/reciprocal wills**: Two people agree on coordinated testamentary plans
- **Contract concerning a joint will**: Two or more people execute a single document containing multiple wills

### 16.2 Joint vs. Mutual Wills

**Mutual wills** consist of two separate instruments executed by two different testators, with each will containing reciprocal provisions. For example, a husband and wife each execute separate wills, with the husband's will leaving everything to the wife, and the wife's will leaving everything to the husband.

**Joint will** is a single instrument that contains the wills of two or more persons. One document serves as the will for multiple testators. Joint wills may be mutual if they contain reciprocal provisions.

Joint wills should generally be avoided because they create numerous practical and legal problems. They can breed litigation on a wide range of issues involving interpretation, revocation, and enforceability.

### 16.3 Presumption (Some Jurisdictions)

In some jurisdictions, mutual wills with reciprocal provisions are **presumed** to be contractual. This means if a husband and wife execute separate but reciprocal wills (each leaving everything to the other, with identical provisions for what happens if both die), the law presumes they made a contract not to revoke those wills.

This presumption can be rebutted by evidence that the parties did not intend their wills to be contractual. However, the presumption shifts the burden to the party claiming no contract existed.

### 16.4 Remedy

When a party breaches a contract concerning a will, the typical remedy is to impose a **constructive trust** on the property that should have passed according to the contract. The constructive trust prevents unjust enrichment of those who benefited from the breach at the expense of those who were supposed to benefit under the contract.

**Example**: Husband and Wife execute mutual wills, each leaving everything to the other, with remainder to their combined children. Husband dies first. Wife then executes a new will leaving everything to only her biological children, cutting out Husband's children from a prior marriage. If a court finds the original wills were contractual, it can impose a constructive trust requiring Wife's estate to distribute property according to the original plan.

The remedy of constructive trust recognizes that while the law cannot force a testator to execute a particular will (testamentary freedom is paramount), it can prevent the unjust enrichment that results from breaking a contractual promise.

## §17. INTERPRETATION & REFORMATION

<aside>
✅

**Quick Checklist**

- **Patent ambiguity** (apparent from face)?
- **Latent ambiguity** (discovered by extrinsic evidence)?
- Can **extrinsic evidence** resolve?
- **Reformation** possible (clear and convincing evidence of mistake + actual intent)?
</aside>

### 17.1 Ambiguity Types

**Patent Ambiguity**: An ambiguity that is apparent from the document's face. Reading the will alone, without any external information, reveals that the language is unclear or susceptible to multiple interpretations.

Example: "I leave my property to Sarah and John equally or to Sarah alone, whichever I decide." This is patently ambiguous because the will itself presents conflicting options without indicating which the testator chose.

**Latent Ambiguity**: An ambiguity that is discovered only by considering extrinsic evidence. The will appears clear on its face, but when you try to apply it to actual facts, an ambiguity emerges.

Example: "I leave $10,000 to my niece Sarah Smith." The will seems clear, but extrinsic evidence reveals the testator had two nieces named Sarah Smith. This latent ambiguity is discovered only when applying the will to real-world facts.

**Traditional rule**: Extrinsic evidence was admissible to resolve latent ambiguities but not patent ambiguities. The reasoning was that patent ambiguities could and should be resolved by examining the four corners of the document.

**Modern trend**: Most courts now admit extrinsic evidence to resolve both patent and latent ambiguities. The Restatement Third of Property endorses this modern approach, allowing courts to consider all relevant evidence to determine the donor's intention.

### 17.2 Key Case

**In re Estate of Gibbs**

**Rule**: Extrinsic evidence is admissible to determine whether property or a beneficiary has been erroneously identified. If extrinsic evidence shows erroneous details in the description, those details should be disregarded to avoid frustrating the testator's intent.

**Facts**: George and Lena Gibbs's wills each made a 1% bequest to "Robert J. Krause" at a specified street address. The Gibbses' long-term friend was Robert W. Krause (different middle initial), who lived at a different address. When both testators died, the trial court awarded the bequests to Robert W. Krause. Robert J. Krause appealed.

**Holding**: Extrinsic evidence properly showed that despite the erroneous middle initial and wrong address, the testators intended to benefit their friend Robert W. Krause. The erroneous identifying details should be disregarded.

**Reasoning**: Although courts traditionally subscribe to an inflexible rule against reformation of wills, they have often strained interpretation in matters of identification to reach a desired result. An ambiguity exists when there is difficulty identifying the person whose name and description correspond with the will's terms—not when extrinsic proof tends to show an intention different from what the will manifests.

Here, the county court properly disregarded the middle initial and street address and determined that Robert W. Krause was the person the testators had in mind. The question is usually framed as whether extrinsic evidence is admissible, but the trial court typically hears it, and the strength of the evidence may determine the outcome.

**Lesson**: Courts distinguish between interpretation (figuring out what ambiguous language means) and reformation (changing what clear language says). Courts more readily "interpret" by admitting extrinsic evidence than they do "reform" by rewriting clear but mistaken language.

### 17.3 Interpretation or Reformation

Courts faced with mistakes in drafting often purport to "interpret" or "construe" a document to correct a mistake, rather than openly "reforming" it. This is because traditional rules preclude reforming wills.

**Restatement Third of Property § 12.1**: A donative document, though unambiguous, may be reformed to conform the text to the donor's intention if the following are established by clear and convincing evidence: (1) a mistake of fact or law, whether in expression or inducement, and (2) the donor's actual intention.

**Restatement Third of Property § 12.2**: A donative document may be modified in a manner that does not violate the donor's probable intention to achieve the donor's tax objectives. This is traditional "tax reformation," which courts have accepted more readily than other forms of reformation, presumably because it involves adjusting the will to achieve objectives the testator clearly had (minimizing taxes) rather than rewriting substantive dispositions.

### 17.4 Critical Case

**Flannery v. McNamara**

**Background**: Massachusetts, 2000

**Facts**: William's will left all property to his wife Katherine. Katherine predeceased William. The will did not name contingent beneficiaries and did not contain a residuary clause. Helen Flannery and Margaret Moran (Katherine's sisters) filed a petition for reformation, claiming they had a close relationship with William and he told them his house and belongings would be theirs someday.

**Issue**: Can the court reform an unambiguous will to fill a gap?

**Holding**: No. Courts do not reform unambiguous wills to fill gaps.

**Reasoning**: While courts construe ambiguous terms in a will, where the will is unambiguous, reformation is not permitted. Reformation of unambiguous wills would open the judicial system to an onslaught of reformation claims from persons who think, however baselessly, that the decedent intended to leave property to them.

Where a will is unambiguous, extrinsic evidence is not admissible to construe it. The will here is unambiguous—it leaves everything to Katherine. The problem is not ambiguity but rather a gap (no provision for if Katherine predeceases).

Reformation is intended to fix mistakes in expression, not to fill gaps. If William had written "I leave everything to Helen and Margaret" but the attorney mistakenly typed "I leave everything to Katherine," reformation might be appropriate. But here, William did not express an intent to benefit Helen and Margaret; he simply failed to provide for the contingency of Katherine's death.

**Lesson**: Reformation corrects mistakes in documenting intent that existed. It does not supply intent that was never formed or expressed. Gap-filling is for interpretation doctrines like lapsed gifts and anti-lapse statutes, not reformation.

## §18. PROTECTIVE PLANNING

### 18.1 No-Contest Clauses

A no-contest clause (also called an "in terrorem" clause) is a provision in a will that forfeits a beneficiary's gift if the beneficiary contests the will. The purpose is to deter will contests by creating a financial disincentive.

**How it works**: "If any beneficiary under this will contests this will or any provision of it, that beneficiary shall forfeit any gift they would otherwise receive under this will."

**Key limitation**: If the will contest succeeds, the no-contest clause falls with the will. A successful contestant loses nothing because the will (including the forfeiture provision) is invalidated.

**Probable cause exception**: Many jurisdictions refuse to enforce no-contest clauses if the contestant had probable cause to bring the contest. Under this approach, even if the contestant loses, they retain their gift if they had reasonable grounds for challenging the will.

The reasoning is that no-contest clauses should deter frivolous challenges, not legitimate ones. If a beneficiary has good reason to believe the will is invalid (evidence of lack of capacity, undue influence, etc.), they should be free to raise that challenge without fear of forfeiture.

### 18.2 Preventive Lawyering

Attorneys can build a record during will execution that deters future contests and strengthens the will's position if challenged:

**1. Video recording of execution ceremony**: A video showing the testator's demeanor, understanding, and voluntary execution provides powerful evidence of capacity and absence of undue influence. The video should show:

- The testator clearly stating their intent to execute their will
- The testator appearing alert and oriented
- The testator answering questions demonstrating understanding of their property and beneficiaries
- The testator executing the will without pressure or coercion from others

**2. Independent legal advice**: The testator should consult with their own attorney, not an attorney selected or paid by a beneficiary. The attorney should meet with the testator privately, without beneficiaries present. The attorney can later testify about the testator's capacity, understanding, and intent.

**3. Physician evaluation letter**: If capacity concerns exist, obtain a letter from the testator's physician contemporaneous with execution, documenting the testator's mental state. The physician can perform a capacity evaluation and document findings.

**4. Public execution**: Execute the will at a bank, attorney's office, or other neutral, professional location rather than at home. Public execution with independent witnesses makes undue influence more difficult and less likely. This was the critical factor in Saucier.

**5. Explanation letter**: The testator might write a letter explaining dispositions that deviate from equal treatment of children or natural objects of bounty. However, this is risky. If the testator gets facts wrong or makes disparaging statements, it can provide ammunition for capacity or libel claims. Use cautiously.

**6. Multiple witnesses**: Include more witnesses than statutorily required. If two witnesses are required, have three or four. This provides backup if some witnesses later become unavailable or have memory problems.

**7. Self-proving affidavit**: Lock in witness testimony through a self-proving affidavit executed contemporaneously with the will. This eliminates the need for witnesses to testify at probate and preserves their contemporaneous recollection.

**8. Attorney's contemporaneous notes**: The attorney should keep detailed notes of meetings with the testator, documenting:

- The testator's understanding of their property
- The testator's knowledge of family members
- The testator's reasons for dispositions
- The testator's mental clarity and decision-making capacity
- Any concerns raised and how they were addressed

These prophylactic measures create a strong evidentiary record that makes successful challenges less likely and protects the testator's intent.

## §20. EXAM WRITING TIPS

### For Will Validity Questions

1. **Always check harmless error (UPC 2-503)** if any formal defect exists. Even if the will fails under traditional execution rules, harmless error may save it.
2. **State the burden and standard**: "The proponent has the burden of proving due execution by a preponderance of the evidence." "The contestant has the burden of proving lack of capacity by a preponderance of the evidence."
3. **Distinguish capacity from volition (UI)**: Make explicit that these are separate doctrines. A testator can have perfect capacity (understanding) while being unduly influenced (lack of free will).

### For UI Questions

1. **Follow the 5-step checklist** in §4: (1) valid will → (2) confidential relationship → (3) suspicious circumstances → (4) burden shifts → (5) defenses
2. **Count factors explicitly**: "The first suspicious circumstance is beneficiary participation in procurement. Here, the facts show..." Work through all six factors systematically.
3. **Explain burden-shifting in steps**: "Initially, the contestant bears the burden of proving confidential relationship and suspicious circumstances by a preponderance. Once proven, a presumption of undue influence arises and the burden shifts to the proponent to prove by clear and convincing evidence..."
4. **Address defenses**: Even if the presumption arises, analyze whether the proponent can rebut it through independent advice, public execution, capacity witnesses, etc.

### For Holographic Questions

1. **Check if jurisdiction allows holographs**: Don't spend time on holographic analysis if the jurisdiction doesn't recognize them.
2. **Identify material portions in handwriting**: Quote the handwritten portions and explain why they constitute "material portions" (dispositive language + beneficiaries).
3. **Consider extrinsic evidence for intent (UPC 2-502(c))**: Can use non-handwritten portions and surrounding circumstances to prove testamentary intent.
4. **Evaluate harmless error as backup**: If holographic requirements aren't quite satisfied, argue harmless error under 2-503.

### For Revocation Questions

1. **Identify method** (writing vs. physical act): The method determines which rules apply.
2. **If physical act: on original? Intent?**: These are the critical elements. Acts on copies don't work; accidental damage doesn't revoke.
3. **Lost will presumption if applicable**: If the will was last in testator's possession but can't be found, apply the presumption and analyze whether it can be rebutted.
4. **Consider DRR if new will failed**: If testator destroyed Will I thinking Will II was valid but Will II failed, DRR may restore Will I.
5. **Consider revival if multiple wills**: Apply UPC 2-509's different rules depending on method of revocation.
