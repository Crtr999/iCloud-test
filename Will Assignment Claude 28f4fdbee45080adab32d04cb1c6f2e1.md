### **SAM'S BEST ARGUMENT: VALID HOLOGRAPHIC CODICIL**

Our best argument is that Bob's October 1, 2015 modifications constitute a valid **holographic codicil** under UPC 2-502(b) and (c), which amended his 2004 will to include Sam as an equal beneficiary with Sally.

### **LEGAL FRAMEWORK: HOLOGRAPHIC WILLS UNDER THE UPC**

UPC 2-502(b) provides that a will is valid as a holographic will if:

1. The signature and
2. Material portions of the document
3. Are in the testator's handwriting

Under UPC 2-502(c), intent that the document constitute a will can be established by extrinsic evidence, including portions of the document that are not in the testator's handwriting.

Crucially, UPC 1-201(56) defines "will" to include any testamentary instrument that "revokes or revises another will" - this encompasses codicils.

### **APPLICATION: HOLOGRAPHIC CODICIL REQUIREMENTS**

**1. Material Portions in Testator's Handwriting**

Bob's handwritten additions constitute the material portions:

- He crossed out "Sally"
- He wrote "Sally and Sam, equally" in his own handwriting above the strikethrough
- He signed his name below the witnesses

The **material portions** are those that express the testamentary intent and the dispositive plan. Here, the handwritten language completely captures Bob's testamentary intent: to change the beneficiary from Sally alone to Sally and Sam equally. This is materially sufficient.

The fact that the underlying document is typed and is a copy of the 2004 will does not defeat the holographic nature. Under UPC 2-502(c), **extrinsic evidence (including non-handwritten portions) can establish intent**. The typed portions showing the will's structure and the express revocation clause help establish that Bob intended this to be a testamentary instrument, not mere musings.

**2. Signature**

Bob signed and dated the document on October 1, 2015, below his previous 2004 signature. This signature is clearly in Bob's handwriting and appears at a logical concluding point of the modifications.

**3. Testamentary Intent**

We can establish testamentary intent through:

**a) The handwritten words themselves**: "Sally and Sam, equally" is dispositive language showing Bob's intent to give property. He's not just making notes - he's effectuating a change to his testamentary plan.

**b) The context**: Bob made these changes to a document that was already a will. He crossed out one name and substituted different language, then signed and dated it. This shows clear intent to modify his testamentary plan.

**c) The formality**: Bob had **two witnesses sign** after his signature. While witnesses are not required for holographic wills under UPC 2-502(b), Bob's decision to have witnesses sign demonstrates he understood the solemnity of what he was doing and intended it to have legal effect. This is powerful evidence of testamentary intent.

**d) Extrinsic evidence**: The fact that Steve (the executor named in the will) has admitted this version to probate suggests Bob likely communicated his intent to Steve or others.

**4. Dating**

Bob dated the modifications October 1, 2015. This satisfies any preference for establishing chronology and shows deliberateness.

### **ADDRESSING SALLY'S LIKELY OBJECTIONS**

**Objection 1: "This is just a copy, not an original will"**

**Response**: The UPC does not require that a holographic will be written on a blank sheet of paper. It can be written on any document, including a copy of a prior will. What matters is whether the handwritten portions, combined with extrinsic evidence, demonstrate testamentary intent.

Moreover, holographic wills exist precisely to accommodate informal testamentary acts. The whole point of UPC 2-502(b) is to give effect to handwritten testamentary instruments that lack the formality of attested wills. If Bob had written "I give my residue to Sally and Sam equally" on a napkin and signed it, that could be a valid holographic will. Writing it on a copy of his existing will is actually stronger evidence of testamentary intent because it shows the context of his overall estate plan.

**Objection 2: "Bob didn't have access to the original, so this can't be a valid revocation"**

**Response**: Bob doesn't need to revoke the original to create a valid codicil. A codicil supplements or amends a will without destroying it. What we have here is a holographic codicil that says: "In my 2004 will, where it says 'All the rest, residue, and remainder of my estate to Sally,' I change that to 'Sally and Sam, equally.'"

Under UPC 1-201(56), a "will" includes any testamentary instrument that "revises another will." That's exactly what Bob did here.

Furthermore, the inability to physically access the original doesn't negate testamentary intent. Courts should focus on what Bob intended, not on formalistic barriers. Bob did everything he could: he used the copy he had, made clear handwritten modifications, signed, dated, and had witnesses sign.

**Objection 3: "This creates ambiguity about whether Bob wanted to revoke the whole will"**

**Response**: There's no ambiguity. Bob didn't cross out the entire will or write "this will is revoked." He made a surgical, specific change to one provision. The rest of the 2004 will remains intact:

- The express revocation clause of prior wills: still valid
- Steve as executor: still valid
- All other provisions: still valid

Only the residuary bequest changes from Sally alone to Sally and Sam equally. This is a classic codicil - amending one provision while leaving the rest intact.

### **POLICY SUPPORT**

The UPC's holographic will provisions serve several policies that support our argument:

1. **Respecting testator intent**: Bob clearly wanted Sam to share in the estate. Denying effect to his modifications based on the technicality that he used a copy would defeat his intent.
2. **Accommodating informality**: Holographic will statutes recognize that testators often cannot or do not comply with strict formalities. Bob did more than required - he even obtained witnesses.
3. **Avoiding intestacy**: If we don't give effect to Bob's modifications, we risk partially intestate situations or frustrating testator intent.

### **ALTERNATIVE/FALLBACK ARGUMENT: HARMLESS ERROR (UPC 2-503)**

If the court is reluctant to accept the holographic codicil theory, we have a strong fallback under UPC 2-503's harmless error doctrine.

UPC 2-503 provides that although a document was not executed in compliance with UPC 2-502, the document is treated as if it had been executed if the proponent establishes by **clear and convincing evidence** that the decedent intended the document to constitute the decedent's will or a revocation, amendment, or addition to the will.

**Application**:

- Bob's handwritten modifications, signature, and date on October 1, 2015
- The presence of two witnesses who signed (showing Bob sought formality)
- The specificity of the modification ("Sally and Sam, equally")
- The time elapsed (Bob lived for months/years after making the modification with no indication he regretted or wanted to undo it)

Together, these provide clear and convincing evidence that Bob intended these modifications to amend his will.

*In re Estate of Wiltfong* illustrates the harmless error doctrine's application to informal writings. There, a typed letter in a greeting card plus contemporaneous statements sufficed. Our case is stronger - Bob actually signed with witnesses.

### **CONCLUSION**

Sam's best argument is that Bob's October 1, 2015 modifications constitute a valid holographic codicil under UPC 2-502(b) and (c). The handwritten portions contain the material testamentary content (signature and dispositive language), and extrinsic evidence (including the typed portions and witness signatures) establishes testamentary intent. The modifications should be given effect, resulting in Sally and Sam sharing the residue equally. Alternatively, under UPC 2-503's harmless error doctrine, the clear and convincing evidence of Bob's intent warrants treating the modifications as a valid amendment even if formal defects exist.
